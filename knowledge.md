## Mitosis vs meiosis: what is the difference?
Mitosis makes two genetically identical daughter cells from one parent cell. It has one round of division and the daughter cells are diploid (2n), the same as the parent. Cells use it for growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction. Meiosis makes four genetically different cells from one parent cell. It has two rounds of division (meiosis I and meiosis II) and the daughter cells are haploid (n). Cells use it to make gametes (sperm and eggs). Only meiosis has synapsis (homologous chromosomes pairing up) and crossing over. Quick memory trick: Mitosis = Make identical copies. Meiosis = Make sex cells with a Mix of genes.

## Stages of mitosis (PMAT)
Before mitosis, the cell copies its DNA during S phase of interphase. Then remember PMAT. Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible, and the spindle starts to form. Metaphase: chromosomes line up along the middle of the cell, called the metaphase plate. Anaphase: sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite ends of the cell. Telophase: nuclear envelopes re-form around the two sets of chromosomes and the chromosomes relax. Cytokinesis, the splitting of the cytoplasm, usually overlaps with telophase and makes two separate cells.

## Stages of meiosis I and meiosis II
DNA is copied once, before meiosis I. Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes. In prophase I, homologs pair up and cross over. In metaphase I, homologous pairs line up together at the middle. In anaphase I, the homologs are pulled apart, but sister chromatids stay together. Telophase I and cytokinesis give two haploid cells. Meiosis II separates sister chromatids and works like mitosis. There is no DNA copying between meiosis I and II. The final result is four haploid cells, each with single-chromatid chromosomes.

## Homologous chromosomes
Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes, one from each parent, that carry the same genes in the same order. They are the same size and shape, but they can carry different versions of a gene, called alleles. Humans have 23 pairs of homologous chromosomes, for 46 in total. Homologs are not identical copies of each other. Sister chromatids are different: they are exact copies made by DNA replication and are joined at the centromere. In meiosis I, homologs pair up and then separate. In meiosis II and mitosis, sister chromatids separate.

## Sex chromosomes vs autosomes
Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes (non-sex chromosomes) and 1 pair of sex chromosomes. Females typically have two X chromosomes (XX) and males typically have an X and a Y (XY). The X and Y are different sizes and carry different genes, but they pair up at small matching regions during meiosis so they can separate properly. Every egg carries an X chromosome. A sperm carries either an X or a Y, so the sperm determines the sex of the offspring. Genes on the X chromosome are called X-linked genes.

## How does meiosis create genetic variation?
Meiosis produces variety in three ways. First, crossing over happens in prophase I, when homologous chromosomes swap pieces of DNA and make new combinations of alleles. Second, independent assortment happens in metaphase I, when each homologous pair lines up and separates independently of the other pairs. With 23 pairs, humans can make over 8 million different chromosome combinations in a gamete. Third, random fertilization means any sperm can combine with any egg. Together these make siblings different from each other and from their parents.

## Haploid vs diploid (n vs 2n)
Haploid (n) means a cell has one set of chromosomes. Diploid (2n) means a cell has two sets, one from each parent. In humans, body cells (somatic cells) are diploid with 46 chromosomes, and gametes are haploid with 23 chromosomes. Meiosis cuts the chromosome number in half, going from diploid to haploid. Fertilization joins two haploid gametes to make a diploid zygote, which restores the full number.

## Steps of cellular respiration
Cellular respiration turns glucose and oxygen into ATP, carbon dioxide, and water. It has four stages. 1) Glycolysis, in the cytoplasm: glucose is split into 2 pyruvate, with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH. 2) Pyruvate oxidation, in the mitochondrial matrix: pyruvate becomes acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2 and making NADH. 3) Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle), in the matrix: acetyl-CoA is broken down, making CO2, a little ATP, NADH, and FADH2. 4) Electron transport chain and chemiosmosis, on the inner mitochondrial membrane: NADH and FADH2 drop off electrons, oxygen accepts them to make water, and most of the ATP is made. The total is roughly 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, depending on the textbook.

## How to remember the steps of cellular respiration
Use the sentence "Go Play Kickball Everyday" for Glycolysis, Pyruvate oxidation, Krebs cycle, Electron transport chain. Then attach one location and one fact to each step. Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm and needs no oxygen. Pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle happen in the matrix. The electron transport chain happens on the inner membrane, and oxygen is the final electron acceptor. Try drawing a mitochondrion from memory and labeling where each stage happens. Then say out loud what goes in and what comes out of each stage.

## Steps of DNA replication
Replication starts at an origin of replication. 1) Helicase unwinds and unzips the double helix, forming a replication fork. 2) Single-strand binding proteins keep the strands apart, and topoisomerase relieves twisting ahead of the fork. 3) Primase makes a short RNA primer. 4) DNA polymerase adds new nucleotides to the primer, always building in the 5' to 3' direction. The leading strand is made continuously. The lagging strand is made in short pieces called Okazaki fragments. 5) Another DNA polymerase replaces the RNA primers with DNA. 6) DNA ligase seals the gaps between fragments. Enzyme names here are from bacteria, and eukaryotes use different polymerases for the same jobs.

## Why is DNA replication called semiconservative?
Semiconservative means each new DNA molecule is made of one old (original) strand and one newly made strand. The original double helix unwinds, and each old strand acts as a template for a new partner strand. This was shown by the Meselson-Stahl experiment, which used nitrogen isotopes (heavy 15N and light 14N) to tell old DNA from new DNA. Two other models were ruled out: conservative replication (one all-old molecule and one all-new molecule) and dispersive replication (mixed pieces of old and new in both strands).

## How to read a metabolic pathway diagram
Start by finding the first molecule (the substrate) and the final molecule (the product). Each arrow is one reaction, and the enzyme is usually written next to the arrow. Read the arrows in order and ask what goes in and what comes out at each step. If an enzyme is missing or blocked, the molecule just before the block builds up and the molecules after it drop. Watch for feedback inhibition, where the final product shuts down an enzyme early in the pathway so the cell doesn't overproduce it. Dotted lines or T-shaped arrows often show inhibition. Always check whether the question asks what happens upstream or downstream of the change.

## Best ways to study and remember biology
Active recall works better than rereading: close your notes and write or say everything you remember, then check what you missed. Spaced repetition means reviewing in short sessions over several days instead of cramming. Teach the concept out loud as if explaining it to a friend. Draw processes like mitosis, meiosis, and respiration from memory instead of copying diagrams. Make mnemonics and analogies for ordered lists like the stages of mitosis. Practice with questions, and write down why each wrong answer is wrong.

## What Is Biology? The Study of Life
Biology is the scientific study of living organisms and how they interact with one another and their environments. Living systems share several common characteristics: they are made of cells, use energy, maintain internal conditions, grow and develop, respond to their surroundings, reproduce, and evolve over generations. Biologists study life at different levels of organization, from molecules and cells to organisms, populations, ecosystems, and the biosphere. These levels are connected: processes at a smaller level can influence what happens at larger levels. Biology uses evidence and testable explanations to understand how living systems work.

## Scientific Method: Hypotheses, Experiments, and Variables
Scientific investigations begin with observations or questions about the natural world. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that can be tested and potentially rejected. A prediction describes an expected result if the hypothesis is correct. In an experiment, the independent variable is the factor a researcher changes, while the dependent variable is the factor measured. Controlled variables are kept consistent so they do not provide alternative explanations. A control group or comparison condition helps researchers interpret results. Good experiments use clear procedures, sufficient sample sizes, and repeatable measurements. Scientific conclusions are based on evidence; a hypothesis is not proven permanently but may be supported by repeated testing.

## Atoms, Molecules, and Chemical Bonds in Biology
All matter is made of atoms, which contain protons, neutrons, and electrons. Atoms can join through chemical bonds to form molecules. Covalent bonds involve sharing electrons, while ionic bonds involve attraction between oppositely charged ions. Hydrogen bonds are weaker attractions that are especially important in water and in the structure of biological molecules such as DNA and proteins. An atom's chemical behavior depends largely on its electrons, especially those in its outer shell. In living organisms, chemical reactions rearrange atoms and bonds; matter is conserved even as substances are transformed.

## Water Properties and pH
Water is polar: its oxygen end has a slight negative charge and its hydrogen ends have slight positive charges. This polarity allows water molecules to form hydrogen bonds with one another and with other polar substances. Water is an effective solvent for many ions and polar molecules, helping substances move and react in cells. Cohesion helps water molecules stick together, while adhesion helps them interact with other surfaces. Water also resists rapid temperature change because hydrogen bonds absorb or release heat. The pH scale describes hydrogen ion concentration: values below 7 are acidic, 7 is neutral, and values above 7 are basic in standard aqueous conditions. Buffers help resist sudden pH changes.

## Biological Macromolecules: Carbohydrates, Lipids, Proteins, and Nucleic Acids
Four major classes of biological macromolecules are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Carbohydrates include sugars and starches and commonly provide quick energy or structural support. Lipids include fats, oils, phospholipids, and steroids; they store energy and help form cell membranes. Proteins are made from amino acids and perform many jobs, including catalysis, transport, signaling, and structural support. Nucleic acids, including DNA and RNA, are built from nucleotides and store or help use genetic information. The shape and chemical properties of each molecule influence its function. Cells build larger molecules by joining smaller subunits and break them down through hydrolysis.

## Cell Theory and the Difference Between Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells
Cell theory states that all living organisms are made of one or more cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and cells arise from preexisting cells. Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria and archaea, do not have a membrane-bound nucleus; their DNA is located in a region called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. Both cell types contain DNA, ribosomes, cytoplasm, and a plasma membrane. Eukaryotic organelles divide cellular tasks: for example, the nucleus stores most genetic information, mitochondria carry out much of aerobic cellular respiration, and ribosomes build proteins.

## Cell Membrane Structure and Transport Across Membranes
The plasma membrane is mainly a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head that interacts with water and hydrophobic tails that avoid water. The membrane is selectively permeable, meaning some substances cross more easily than others. Small nonpolar molecules can often diffuse through the lipid bilayer, while ions and many polar molecules need transport proteins. Passive transport, including diffusion and osmosis, moves substances down their concentration or water-potential gradients without direct energy input. Active transport uses cellular energy, often ATP, to move substances against a gradient. Membrane transport helps cells maintain homeostasis.

## Enzymes and How They Affect Chemical Reactions
Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that speed up chemical reactions by lowering activation energy. They are not used up by the reactions they catalyze. A substrate binds to an enzyme's active site, where the reaction occurs and products are released. Enzyme activity can be affected by temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and inhibitors. Conditions that are too extreme can alter an enzyme's shape and reduce its function; this loss of shape is called denaturation. Enzymes are often specific to particular substrates or reactions. By controlling reaction rates, enzymes help cells carry out metabolism efficiently under conditions compatible with life.

## Cellular Respiration: How Cells Release Energy from Food
Cellular respiration transfers energy from molecules such as glucose into ATP, the main energy-carrying molecule used by cells. In aerobic respiration, oxygen is used and carbon dioxide and water are produced. The main stages are glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and splits glucose into smaller molecules, producing a small amount of ATP and electron carriers. In eukaryotic cells, later stages occur in mitochondria, where electrons moving through the electron transport chain help drive ATP production. Cells can also make ATP through fermentation when oxygen is unavailable, although this yields much less ATP per glucose molecule.

## Photosynthesis: How Plants Convert Light Energy into Chemical Energy
Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules. In plants, it occurs in chloroplasts. The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes, where light energy drives the production of ATP and NADPH and water is split, releasing oxygen. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma and uses carbon dioxide, ATP, and NADPH to build carbohydrate molecules. The overall process connects energy from sunlight to the chemical energy that supports many food webs. Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are related but are not exact reverses: each consists of multiple regulated reactions and serves a different role in cells.

## DNA, Genes, and the Flow of Genetic Information
DNA is a double-stranded molecule made of nucleotides. Its bases pair specifically: adenine with thymine, and cytosine with guanine. A gene is a DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product, often a protein or functional RNA. During DNA replication, each original strand serves as a template for a new strand, allowing genetic information to be copied before cell division. In gene expression, transcription makes an RNA copy of a DNA sequence, and translation uses the information in messenger RNA to assemble a polypeptide at a ribosome. Mutations are changes in DNA sequence; their effects can be neutral, harmful, or beneficial depending on context.

## Mitosis, Meiosis, and Inheritance
The cell cycle includes growth, DNA replication, and division. Mitosis divides a eukaryotic cell's duplicated chromosomes into two nuclei, usually producing two genetically similar daughter cells. It supports growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction in some organisms. Meiosis involves two rounds of cell division and produces haploid cells, such as gametes in animals. It reduces chromosome number by half and creates genetic variation through crossing over and independent assortment. During fertilization, two haploid gametes combine to form a diploid zygote. Inheritance describes how genetic information passes from parents to offspring; alleles are different versions of a gene, and genotype can influence observable phenotype.

## Evolution by Natural Selection and the Evidence for Evolution
Evolution is change in the inherited characteristics of populations across generations. Natural selection occurs when individuals vary in heritable traits and some variants consistently contribute to greater reproductive success in a particular environment. Over generations, advantageous inherited traits can become more common in a population. Individuals do not evolve during their lifetimes; populations evolve across generations. Evidence for evolution includes fossils, similarities in anatomy and DNA, the geographic distribution of species, and directly observed changes in populations. Natural selection is one mechanism of evolution, alongside processes such as mutation, genetic drift, and gene flow. Evolution explains both the diversity of life and the relatedness of organisms.

## Ecology: Populations, Communities, Ecosystems, and Energy Flow
Ecology studies how organisms interact with one another and with their physical environment. A population consists of individuals of the same species in an area. A community includes the populations of different species, while an ecosystem includes the community and its nonliving environment. Producers, such as plants and algae, capture energy, usually from sunlight. Consumers obtain energy by eating other organisms, and decomposers break down dead material and recycle nutrients. Energy flows through food chains and food webs, with some energy lost as heat at each transfer. Matter, including carbon and nitrogen, cycles between organisms and the environment. Population size is influenced by resources, competition, predation, disease, and environmental conditions.
